Idle — back to start

No empty promises

What works, what we’re building and what’s missing

The “not available” section sits alongside the rest — not tucked away in the footer.

  • You sort photos, videos and PDFs into folders the way the kiosk app should be structured, then connect the root folder. Screens, transitions and names are built from the folder structure.

    Three days of manual setup turn into a review of a ready-made tree.

  • Server builds are fully written: after payment, images upload to the server and are read from storage one at a time, the archive is signed, and the kiosk checks the signature at startup and tells you if the content was tampered with. What’s left is running the whole path from payment to kiosk on a live setup.

    We can say “works” only after a live run, not because the checks are green.

  • Changed two photos out of forty — only those two go to the server. The rest comes from the package already built.

    Editing a large project no longer depends on your internet speed.

  • The app runs on the system WebView2 engine. Windows 7 and 8 don’t have it and won’t: their support ended in January 2023.

    A Windows 10 mini PC costs about as much as a single project.

  • Catalog or menu, trade-show booth, sales office. A project type is a recipe for naming your folders, not someone else’s template: the same import sorts them by that recipe.

    Your first ten minutes don’t start with a blank canvas.

  • An invoice is issued to the client’s company details, and we mark the payment as received. A form with our own company details isn’t ready yet.

    Companies pay by invoice.

  • A model looks at the image, finds areas that look like buttons, reads their labels and suggests ready-made markup.

    A person still reviews it: it’s a suggestion, not a decision.

  • To avoid uploading your gigabytes to a server, the editor reads files straight from your disk. In Firefox and Safari, folder access is lost after a page reload.

    It still works, but you’ll have to pick the folder again.

  • The built app makes no network requests at all. A test checks this on every build.

    The kiosk can stand where there’s no network and never will be.

  • On a developer machine it’s 373 ms against a two-second budget. Still to be measured on a real kiosk.

    A build on a reference machine measures it, not a person by eye.

  • Reports by date already exist and already download as a file; next comes a “before and after” comparison around a change to the kiosk app.

    Management wants to see the difference, not a snapshot of one month.

  • Two people work through proposals, not locks: the co-author proposes, the owner approves. There’s no live shared cursor on one screen.

    That way the kiosk app keeps one person in charge.

  • An algorithm lays out the tree. Nodes can’t be dragged with the mouse, so a stray move can’t break the structure.

    The largest real project on this layout has 216 screens, and it lays out within the time budget.

  • Service screens, banners, PIN exit, the update prompt and the kiosk system windows follow the Windows language, Russian or English; the language can also be set explicitly. Project texts are in one language for now, and apartment statuses in the 3D scene stay in Russian. Not yet run on a kiosk with English Windows.

    The kiosk can stand at a trade show abroad or in a hotel whose staff do not read Russian.

  • A kiosk with a camera. A visitor takes a photo, picks a frame and prints it or picks it up via a QR code.

    Requested by shopping malls, event organizers and kiosk rental companies.

  • A document of three hundred pages or more takes several minutes to rasterize. It’s a one-time step, but a noticeable one.

    We warn you about it before processing starts.

  • The browser runs exactly the code that ships to the kiosk. Not a look-alike preview — the player itself.

    No “it looked different in preview” surprises.

  • Transcoding for your kiosk’s processor, not for some average machine. The worker is written and isolated; a run on the reference machine is still ahead.

    Proven by playing the video, not by a promise.

  • Receipt and photo printers, QR and barcode scanners, RFID readers, a presence sensor to leave the attract screen, a payment terminal.

    Each device is a module with a clear set of actions, not custom code for one client.

  • The free in-browser build hits the engine’s limit — two gigabytes in one archive. Your computer’s memory has nothing to do with it. The editor tells you in the export window before you click; apps that big are built on the server.

    Real kiosk apps with video weigh around two gigabytes, and keeping quiet about the limit would mean letting you fail at the last step.

  • The file has a version number and a chain of migrations. A project built today will open in future versions of the editor.

    You’re not locked in with us forever: the file is human-readable.

  • The author clicks “Prepare USB stick”, a staff member plugs it in and restarts the kiosk. The kiosk checks the signature and every file, checks there’s enough space, asks, and replaces the contents entirely; if power fails mid-update, it finishes or rolls back on the next start. The archive includes instructions and an autostart file: after the computer starts or crashes, the screen shows the kiosk app again, not the desktop. Written and covered by checks; a run on a real kiosk is still ahead.

    Until we’ve run it live, this is exactly what we promise — not “works”.

  • Screen narration, image descriptions, large type, a low-vision mode.

    For government clients and large chains, this is a contract requirement.

  • Only the website and the request form are in English today. The editor, the dashboard and server messages are in Russian; English arrives with our first international client. Until then, we build such projects for you.

    Better to say so up front than to hand you an interface you can’t read.

  • The project stores a file’s fingerprint, not a copy. Images go to the server only for a build — and each file is stored once, however many screens use it. What you upload and the finished archive are kept for a year after the last build; a draft that was never built is kept for 60 days after the last upload. We’ll remind you twice before the deadline.

    Images that stay on your disk cost nothing. A year of storage for what we hold is included in the build; you can extend it by another year with a button in your account — 60 ₽ per GB of archive and originals, 1 900 ₽ minimum.

  • On a phone you can check the preview and the build status. Marking up buttons with a finger on a small screen is a bad idea, and we won’t pretend otherwise.

    Tablets from ten inches already work.

  • Versions, history, a three-color diff between versions. Open it on another machine and pick up where you left off.

    Your work isn’t tied to one computer.

  • Schedules, queues, stock and prices aren’t pulled in automatically yet.

    On the kiosk this is static content that you update by hand.

  • The owner shares access with a link. A co-author edits the same canvas, but every edit is a proposal: the owner sees the difference and approves it. A co-author can export only an approved version. A company can set up an organization so colleagues can view the team’s projects.

    The contractor prepares, the director signs off — and the product shows who did what.

  • We won’t build walkthroughs from captured points. Instead there’s a true 3D scene: building, floor, apartment. It’s already in the product — see its card under “Works”.

    A panorama can’t change the weather or the time of day and can’t show a floor from above — and that’s exactly what a sales office needs.

  • The director opens the kiosk app in a browser from a link, leaves a comment on a specific screen and clicks “Approve”. No sign-up needed; the approval is bound to the exact markup, not just to its list of contents. Photos appear as downscaled copies — the originals stay on your disk until the build is paid for.

    Fix a year on a screen after approval and the approval no longer applies — and that’s visible.

  • Polls, countdowns, weather and schedules won’t be sold as separate add-ons. Features a kiosk needs come as an app type, not as a shop bolted onto a flat kiosk app.

    A kiosk that takes payments and has a camera is a different product from the same factory, not a screen you drop into a project.

  • A proposal with its own number and validity date, to justify the purchase. The price validity date in it is the same one the payment checks: one date for both sides.

    Developers and retail chains start the conversation with a proposal, and approval takes weeks.

  • The kiosk app is built for Windows. On Linux, the browser-kiosk archive works — the same app opened in a browser. A native kiosk for Russian operating systems and a listing in the Russian software registry will come with the first client on Astra Linux.

    H.264 video playback on Linux isn’t guaranteed without extra packages, and we won’t promise a kiosk we haven’t tested on site.

  • A scene project starts from a single GLB model of the building and a poster. The editor suggests floors and apartments from the names in the model; you set each apartment’s status and details. The preview renders the building with the same player that ships to the kiosk: tap the building to open a floor, tap a floor to open an apartment with its card. Where there’s no graphics card, the kiosk shows the poster with regular buttons. We’re still measuring speed on low-end kiosk PCs.

    A sales office shows buyers the building and the apartment inside it — not a floor plan in a PDF.

  • The kiosk counts how many times a screen was opened and an area was tapped. A sequence like “this person went this way” is never collected: the data holds no visitor identifier at all, and the database schema check fails on any attempt to add one.

    This is a decision, not an oversight: the kiosk stands in a public place and shouldn’t track people.

  • An English version of the site: this landing page, “from $” prices and the request form in English. Our rates are set in rubles, so dollar prices are a guide; we confirm the exact amount and how to pay with the invoice.

    A client abroad reads the offer in their language and requests an invoice without translating the page.

  • Up to 20% replaced is free, then on a sliding scale. The contents at the time of payment are saved and compared with the new version.

    You don’t pay twice for the same thing.

  • Four separate confirmations: the works themselves, photos of them, the people shown, and permission from the museum’s management if the project includes museum objects. A log of acceptances with the date and the document hash.

    A rights holder’s claim goes to the client, not to “someone”.

  • Coordinates are stored in project pixels. One file runs on 1366×768 and on 4K alike.

    Change the kiosk and the app doesn’t need to be redone.

  • In the editor and in the built app. Both are contrast-checked, not picked by eye.

    A screen in a bright lobby and a screen in a dark hall are different jobs.

  • If a 2 GB archive download breaks off, it resumes from where it stopped using the same link. The link stays valid for six hours, not just until the first byte.

    Networks on site can be poor, and downloading two gigabytes again isn’t an option.

  • The kiosk counts which screens were opened and which areas were tapped. The log arrives on a USB stick, is parsed in your browser and goes to the server as an anonymized summary.

    A button nobody ever pressed is your answer on what to fix.

  • Screens nobody opened and areas nobody tapped are listed separately, and first. The period is set by dates, not “the last 30 days”. The report downloads as a file and opens in Excel with non-Latin text intact, not garbled.

    Reports get printed and filed, and a year later “the last 30 days” means nothing.

  • Rights holders’ claims, the build queue and a monthly revenue summary.

    An internal tool, but the service can’t scale without it.
